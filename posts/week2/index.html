Models & Null Models

From “what is there?” to “what could be there?”

This week we explore models and null models. Instead of only describing the Marvel network, we compare it with mathematical models and randomized versions of the network to understand which properties are meaningful and which could arise simply from the network's structure.

Does Marvel Look Random, BA, or Neither?

Research question

Does the real network's degree distribution look more random, more Barabási–Albert (BA), or neither?

Method

We converted the directed hyperlink graph to an undirected graph for neighbourhood comparisons, then compared it with an Erdős–Rényi graph with the same number of nodes and edges and a BA graph with the same number of nodes and a comparable average degree.

Visualization

Figure 1. Toggle Marvel, random, and BA traces; hover for degree and CCDF values, then zoom or pan.

What we found

The random graph has a narrow, thin tail. Marvel has isolates and a much longer tail, while BA reproduces the broad hub-heavy shape more closely. The complementary cumulative distribution function (CCDF) shows the fraction of characters with degree at least k.

What it means

Marvel is more BA-like than random in its degree shape, but “looks like BA” is not evidence that Wikipedia pages actually grew by preferential attachment. A fit alone cannot establish a generative mechanism, causality, directionality, or rule out other heavy-tailed models.

The Friendship Paradox — Are Your Friends More Popular Than You?

Research question

The friendship paradox says that, on average, your friends have more friends than you do. Does that hold for this Marvel network?

Method

For every non-isolated character, we compared degree with the mean degree of their neighbours. “Friend” means an undirected hyperlink neighbour here, not a canonical Marvel friendship.

Visualization

Figure 2. Hover over a character to inspect degree, average neighbour degree, and the difference between them.

What we found

250 of 286 connected characters (87.4%) have neighbours with a higher average degree. The character average is 10.0, compared with 24.2 for the average neighbour degree. Spider-Man and Radian (Morituri) are local maxima: none of their immediate neighbours has a higher degree.

What it means

Unequal degree makes hubs appear in many more neighbourhoods, so edge sampling over-represents popular characters. The strongest individual effects are Enigma, Ethan Edwards, Helix, Phil Grayfield, and Thunderclap: each has degree 1 but a neighbour with degree 106. This is a structural sampling effect, not a claim about social popularity.

What Survives a Degree-Preserving Shuffle?

Research question

Which Marvel properties remain unusual after we preserve each character's directed in-degree and out-degree?

Method

A null model is a comparison world: we repeatedly swapped edge endpoints while preserving every node's in- and out-degree. We measured the real graph and 100 shuffled graphs, then report the null mean, sample standard deviation, and a two-sided empirical p-value.

What it means

Reciprocity and clustering survive the shuffle: who connects to whom contains structure beyond degree. Directed in- and out-degree are fixed, but undirected overlap can change when reciprocal pairs are rewired, so the undirected maximum degree and hub share are still informative here. A null model therefore tells us what kind of structure the test controls for; significance is not a universal property of a graph.

Grow Your Own Marvel — Preferential Attachment

Research question

What does a simple preferential-attachment growth rule get right, and what is the most glaring thing it gets wrong?

Method

We generated a BA network with n = 303 and m = 5. This gives a comparable scale and average degree to the 1,434-edge undirected Marvel graph without pretending that the model has Marvel's page histories or semantics.

Visualization

Figure 4. Interactive ranked degree profiles and CCDFs for Marvel and the BA model.

What we found

The model gets the broad hub-and-many-small-nodes shape right, including a heavy tail. It gets the details wrong: the BA graph is connected and much more cleanly hierarchical, while Marvel contains isolates, reciprocal references, topical structure, and a bumpier degree profile.

What it means

Preferential attachment is a useful baseline for hub formation, not a complete explanation of the network. The most glaring mismatch is that one growth rule cannot reproduce the editorial and community structure visible in the real hyperlink graph.

What Did the Models Teach Us?

The degree distribution is hub-heavy and more BA-like than random, and the friendship paradox is strong because high-degree characters are encountered disproportionately often. Degree-preserving shuffles show that reciprocity and clustering are genuinely more organised than degree alone predicts, while maximum degree is explained by the fixed sequence by construction.

The final lesson is comparative: the degree sequence and a generic growth model explain the existence of hubs, but not the relationships among those hubs. The properties that survive an appropriate null model — especially reciprocal and clustered structure — are the strongest candidates for genuinely structural features of this Marvel network.
